Research Methodology Cheat Sheet

Unit I: Research basics and ethics

Research methodology

  • The systematic study of the methods, techniques and logic used to do research.
  • Nature: systematic, logical, objective, repeatable, goal-oriented.
  • Scope: problem, design, data, analysis, reporting, ethics.
  • Objectives of research: new knowledge, solve problems, new products, improve systems, test ideas.
  • Why it matters: valid and repeatable results, less bias, saves time and cost.

Types of research

  • Basic: new knowledge or theory. Eg: why deep networks generalise.
  • Applied: solve a practical problem. Eg: crop disease detection app.
  • Exploratory: new area, little prior work. Eg: Generative AI in software testing.
  • Experimental: change variables under control and measure the effect. Eg: learning rate vs accuracy.
  • Quick trick: theory = basic, product = applied, “limited studies” = exploratory, “effect of” or “compare” = experimental.

Research process

  • Problem → literature review → gap → objectives and hypothesis → methodology → data collection → analysis → interpretation → report.
  • It is iterative: if objectives are not met, go back to the methodology.
  • ML project workflow: define problem → collect data → preprocess → build model → evaluate → deploy and report.
  • Preprocessing: clean, fill gaps, remove outliers, scale, add features.

Problem, question, objectives

  • Problem = what is wrong. Question = what to find out. Objective = what to do. Methodology = how.
  • Good question: clear, specific, researchable, measurable, relevant, feasible, ethical.
  • Measurable objective has a number. Eg: reach at least 90% accuracy in under 2 seconds.
  • Hypothesis: H₀ = no effect, H₁ = effect.

Research ethics

  • Principles: honesty, public safety, objectivity, confidentiality, respect for intellectual property, accountability.
  • Practices: no fabrication or plagiarism, cite sources, take consent, get ethics approval, keep records.
  • Data issues: consent, privacy, security, bias, purpose limitation.
  • Anonymisation: removing names is not enough. Age, address, admission date and a rare disease are quasi-identifiers (linkage attack). Fix: use ranges, generalise, restrict access.
  • Face data: cannot be changed, so give notice, take consent, limit use, delete after a fixed time.
  • IEEE: public safety first, honesty, avoid conflicts, credit others. ACM: avoid harm, honesty, fairness, privacy, security.

Unit II: Literature, proposals and bibliometrics

Literature review and research gap

  • Steps: keywords → Boolean search (AND, OR) → databases → filters → screen title, abstract, full text → review table → gap.
  • Databases: Google Scholar, Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, CiteSeerX, Shodhganga, ResearchGate.
  • Gap: what earlier work missed, such as small data, no validation or lab-only testing.
  • Gap decides method: no cause shown → experimental; new area → exploratory; lab-only → field study.

Research sources

  • Journal paper: peer-reviewed, detailed, complete validated results.
  • Conference paper: short, fast, recent work.
  • Patent: legal right for an invention; claims and description.
  • Thesis: long degree document with full review (Shodhganga).
  • Book: settled knowledge and basics.
  • Spot in IEEE style: volume and pages = journal; “in Proc.” = conference; “U.S. Patent” = patent; “thesis, Dept., University” = thesis.

Proposal vs paper

  • Proposal parts: title, abstract, background, problem, literature and gap, objectives, scope, methodology, expected outcomes, timeline, budget, ethics, references.
  • Proposal: written before the work, future tense, plan and budget.
  • Paper: written after the work, past tense, actual results.
  • Why each part: title shows topic, question gives focus, objectives are measurable, methodology shows how, budget shows cost.

Bibliometrics

  • Impact Factor = citations in the year to the previous 2 years’ articles ÷ citable articles of those 2 years. Eg: 280 ÷ 120 = 2.33.
  • h-index: largest h such that h papers have at least h citations. Eg: 25, 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 1 gives h = 6.
  • Method: sort in descending order and compare each count with its rank.
  • i10-index: number of papers with at least 10 citations.
  • g-index: largest g such that the top g papers have at least g² citations together.
  • Impact factor is for a journal. h, i10 and g are for an author or group.

Unit III: Statistics, errors and sampling

Hypothesis testing

  • Steps: state H₀ and H₁ → choose α (0.05) → run the test → get the p-value → decide.
  • Rule: p < α: reject H₀ (significant). p ≥ α: fail to reject H₀ (never say “accept”).
  • p-value: chance of a result this extreme if H₀ is true.
  • Type-I error (α): reject a true H₀, a false positive.
  • Type-II error (β): miss a real effect, a false negative. Power = 1 − β.
  • Eg: p = 0.032 → reject. p = 0.051 → fail to reject.

Choosing a test

  • Two group means: t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ÷ [sp √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)], sp² = (SS₁ + SS₂) ÷ (n₁ + n₂ − 2).
  • Paired (same folds): t = d̄ ÷ (sd ÷ √n), df = n − 1. Non-normal differences: Wilcoxon.
  • Three or more means: ANOVA, F = MSB ÷ MSW, MSB = SSB ÷ (k − 1), MSW = SSW ÷ (N − k). Reject H₀ if F is more than the critical F. Then use a post-hoc test.
  • Why not many t-tests: Type-I error rises.
  • Pearson r: r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ √[Σ(x − x̄)² · Σ(y − ȳ)²], from −1 to +1. Eg: study hours vs marks, r = 0.995.
  • Partial correlation: rxy.z = (rxy − rxz ryz) ÷ √[(1 − rxz²)(1 − ryz²)]

Sampling and confidence interval

  • Probability sampling: simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster. Results can be generalised.
  • Non-probability sampling: convenience, purposive, quota, snowball. Quick but may be biased.
  • 95% CI for a mean: x̄ ± 1.96 × s ÷ √n (use t for small n).
  • Eg: mean 85, s = 4, n = 25 gives 85 ± 1.57.
  • A bigger sample gives a narrower interval.

Errors and uncertainty

  • Random error: unpredictable, lowers precision, average many readings.
  • Systematic error: fixed shift, lowers accuracy, fix by calibration.
  • Sources: instrument, environment, observer, method, sampling, computation.
  • Mean = Σx ÷ n. Sample SD s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)]. Standard error = s ÷ √n.
  • Combine errors: sum or difference σ = √(σ₁² + σ₂²); product or quotient: add relative errors in quadrature.
  • Eg: 48, 51, 49, 52, 50 ms gives mean 50, s = 1.58, SE = 0.71.

Multiple testing, Gini and funding

  • Chance of at least one false positive = 1 − (1 − α)m. Eg: m = 20 gives 64%.
  • Fixes: Bonferroni (α ÷ m), Holm, false discovery rate.
  • Gini impurity = 1 − Σp² (lower = purer node). Gini coefficient = inequality, 0 to 1.
  • Research funding: pays for equipment, people, data and publication, and improves planning.

Unit IV: Optimisation

Optimisation basics

  • Find the best values of the decision variables to minimise or maximise an objective, under constraints.
  • Parts: variables, objective function, constraints.
  • Eg: minimise run time T(threads, cache, batch) with threads ≤ CPU cores.
  • Constraints guide the method: bounds → projected methods; equality → Lagrange; inequality → penalty or barrier.

Main methods

  • Sequential uniform sampling: evenly spaced grid, repeatable, cost mn for n variables.
  • Monte Carlo: random points, depends on the seed, good for many variables.
  • Gradient descent: θ ← θ − η ∇J(θ). Newton: x ← x − H⁻¹ ∇f (fast but costly).
  • Batch GD uses all samples; SGD uses one; mini-batch uses a small group (best balance).
  • Direct search needs no derivatives. Gradient-based is fast on smooth functions but may stick in a local minimum.
  • Simplex (2 variables): order B, G, W → centroid M = (B + G) ÷ 2 → reflect R = M + (M − W) → expand E = M + 2(M − W) → contract C = M + 0.5(W − M) → shrink towards B → stop when the triangle is small.

Learning rate, cost and tuning

  • Learning rate η: small = slow, good = fast, large = overshoot or diverge. Eg: J = θ², θ₀ = 4: η = 0.5 reaches 0 in one step; η = 1.1 diverges.
  • Stop when: gradient is near 0, change is small, maximum iterations, or early stopping.
  • MSE = (1 ÷ m) Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)². Sensitive to outliers (Huber is robust). Add λ‖w‖² against overfitting.
  • Local minimum, saddle point, flat region: slow or false convergence. Use momentum, Adam or random restarts.
  • Hyperparameter tuning: grid (all combinations), random (faster), Bayesian (fewest trials), genetic (complex spaces).
  • Eg: best of the table is learning rate 0.05, batch 32 → 96%.
